Two time series can look equally noisy while having very different forecasting properties. One may be independent noise; another may contain strong dependence on yesterday's value. Autocorrelation helps reveal that difference—but it must be interpreted with care.

## What are we trying to detect?

A time plot can reveal trend, seasonality, changing variance, outliers, and structural breaks. A **lag plot** instead compares $y_t$ with $y_{t-k}$, asking whether observations $k$ steps apart tend to move together.

For lag $k$, a common sample ACF estimator is

$$
\hat\rho_k=\frac{\sum_{t=k+1}^{T}(y_t-\bar y)(y_{t-k}-\bar y)}{\sum_{t=1}^{T}(y_t-\bar y)^2}.
$$

By construction, $\hat\rho_0=1$. The ACF is a *linear* dependence summary; it cannot detect every nonlinear or conditional relationship.

## Four useful data-generating processes

**White noise.** If $y_t=\varepsilon_t$ with independent, zero-mean disturbances, its population autocorrelation is zero at nonzero lags. Finite samples still show random spikes.

**AR(1).** Let

$$
y_t=\phi y_{t-1}+\varepsilon_t,\qquad |\phi|<1.
$$

For a stationary AR(1), the population ACF is $\rho_k=\phi^k$. Positive $\phi$ produces a geometrically decaying ACF; negative $\phi$ produces alternating signs.

**Random walk.** Let $y_t=y_{t-1}+\varepsilon_t$. This is not covariance-stationary: its variance grows with time. The sample ACF often decays slowly, but that does not make its level predictable in the same way as a stationary AR process. Its increments are white noise under these assumptions.

**Seasonal signal.** A noisy sinusoid with period twelve exhibits repeated ACF peaks around lags 12, 24, and 36. Real seasonal data may also contain trend and changing seasonal amplitude.

## Why slow ACF decay can be misleading

Suppose a series is a random walk. Its consecutive values are similar because each new observation inherits the previous level. The ACF may therefore look strongly positive for many lags, even though the next *change* is unpredictable from past changes under the independent-increment assumption.

Differencing gives

$$
\Delta y_t=y_t-y_{t-1}=\varepsilon_t.
$$

The differenced series now has no serial correlation at nonzero lags in the ideal model. This is why stationarity and differencing matter before interpreting ACFs or fitting models such as ARIMA.

## Reproduce the experiment in Python

```python
import numpy as np
from statsmodels.graphics.tsaplots import plot_acf
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

rng = np.random.default_rng(4)
n = 160
noise = rng.normal(size=n)

ar = np.empty(n)
ar[0] = noise[0]
for t in range(1, n):
    ar[t] = 0.7 * ar[t - 1] + noise[t]

random_walk = np.cumsum(noise)

fig, axes = plt.subplots(2, 2, figsize=(10, 6))
axes[0, 0].plot(ar)
axes[0, 0].set_title("Stationary AR(1)")
plot_acf(ar, lags=36, ax=axes[0, 1])
axes[1, 0].plot(random_walk)
axes[1, 0].set_title("Random walk")
plot_acf(random_walk, lags=36, ax=axes[1, 1])
plt.tight_layout()
```

## Autocorrelation is not a forecast evaluation

A series with autocorrelation may still be difficult to forecast accurately, especially when structural breaks or changing volatility occur. A convincing forecasting analysis should compare against simple baselines, evaluate on chronologically held-out data, and avoid fitting preprocessing steps using future observations.

For financial returns, small and unstable autocorrelations are especially important: a relationship that looks meaningful in-sample may be too weak to survive transaction costs and changing market regimes.

## Takeaway

ACF is a useful first diagnostic, not proof of forecastability. It helps formulate hypotheses about persistence and seasonality; stationarity checks, residual diagnostics, and honest out-of-sample testing determine whether those hypotheses translate into useful forecasts.
